This plot overlays the raw energy consumption signal (grey line) with the detected anomaly points (red dots) to show exactly 
where and when anomalies occur in the physical energy data.


What the plot shows:
The grey signal reveals the building's characteristic operational pattern — regular sharp daily peaks reaching 10–12 units during
working hours, dropping to near-zero at night and weekends. This is a typical primary school energy profile: high consumption 
during school hours, minimal consumption outside them.


Key observations:
The red anomaly dots cluster in four distinct temporal regions, each corresponding to one of the MSE spikes seen in Diagram 1. 

Looking carefully at where the dots appear:
The anomalies are concentrated at transition zones — specifically at points where the energy drops sharply from a high operating 
state to near-zero, or where the signal exhibits unusual sustained low values between periods of normal high activity. These are 
not occurring at the peaks of consumption (which would indicate overconsumption anomalies) but rather at the valleys and 
boundaries between operating and non-operating states.
This pattern is physically meaningful for a school building. It suggests the model is detecting events such as unexpected early 
shutdowns where energy drops to zero mid-week during what should be a normal operating period, unusually extended low-energy 
periods that break the expected weekly rhythm, and boundary events where the transition between high and low states is sharper 
or slower than the model has learnt as normal.
The fact that regular weekend drops and overnight shutdowns are not flagged as anomalies confirms that the model has correctly 
learnt these periodic patterns as normal behaviour. It is only the irregular or unexpected versions of these transitions that are 
being flagged.
The anomaly at late November 2015 producing the highest MSE (0.050) corresponds to a cluster of red dots that spans a prolonged 
period, suggesting this may have been an extended operational disruption — possibly a building closure, HVAC fault, or maintenance 
period — rather than a brief event.
